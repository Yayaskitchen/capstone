Section 09 · Competency development

Evidence-based self-assessment across five competencies

Proficiency overview

The final ratings use the course's five-level proficiency scale: 1 Fundamental Awareness, 2 Novice, 3 Intermediate, 4 Advanced, and 5 Expert. The ratings are deliberately conservative and evidence-based. Level 5 is not claimed because a single capstone cannot establish recognized authority across organizations. 9.1 · Expertise in Health Equity & Sustainability

Understanding structural drivers, translated into action

START 4 → FINAL 4

This competency requires understanding the structural drivers of inequity and the capacity to translate that understanding into policies, practices, and collaborative action that support equity and sustainability.

EvidenceWhat it demonstrates
Critical literature synthesisUsed James, hooks, and Ani to connect racialization, institutional worldview, voice, care, identity, pathways, and flourishing without locating failure in Black students.
Human-rights and strategy analysisConnected OHRC duties and TVDSB commitments to monitoring, accountability, well-being, belonging, and institutional responsibility.
Prepared for Black Students frameworkDefined flourishing as a health-equity outcome and linked it to relational, community, institutional, and sustainability conditions.
Institution-focused evaluation agendaProposed measures of pathway quality, coach sustainability, family trust, and leadership response — not only student participation or graduation.

Critical reflection

Entering the capstone with advanced experience in health equity and anti-racism, the growth was not primarily a movement to a higher numerical level. It was a transfer and deepening of expertise — learning to treat schools as health-producing systems and to connect educational pathways with stress, identity safety, social connection, opportunity, and future life chances. The capstone sharpened the ability to translate a structural critique into specific duties, implementation conditions, and measures, and to be more disciplined about the difference between naming inequity and demonstrating local outcomes.

Continuing development

The next development task is to test the framework with Black students, families, coaches, community leaders, and institutional decision-makers while preserving community authority over interpretation and use.

9.2 · Systems Thinking & Applied Expertise

From stakeholder lists to a working feedback loop

START 3 → FINAL 4

This competency requires responsiveness to the larger system, recognition of interdependence, and the ability to integrate organizational and community resources into practical solutions.

EvidenceWhat it demonstrates
Systems problem formulationReframed the issue from whether individual students access support to whether the institution is prepared to affirm students and change recurring conditions.
Five-domain frameworkConnected flourishing, relational practice, family and community partnership, institutional transformation, and accountability/sustainability.
Coach-to-leadership feedback loopSpecified how situated knowledge should move through documentation, escalation, ownership, response, improvement, and evaluation.
Implementation and evaluation architectureLinked role charters, pathway monitoring, community voice, affirming spaces, sustainability, and institutional response measures.

Critical reflection

Systems thinking grew from identifying multiple actors to specifying how the parts must interact. A list of stakeholders is not yet a system model. The breakthrough was making the movement of knowledge visible: relationship produces situated knowledge; recurring patterns must be documented; leaders must own a response; institutional practice must change; and the result must be evaluated. This logic prevented the coach from being treated as a stand-alone intervention and prevented institutional accountability from remaining abstract.

Continuing development

Future work should convert the conceptual model into a jointly owned governance map with named decision rights, escalation standards, data stewardship, response timelines, and feedback to students and communities.

9.3 · Critical Reasoning & Creative Problem-Solving

Precision as a form of protection, not retreat

START 3 → FINAL 4

This competency requires identification of the core issue, anticipation of risks, development of defensible solutions, and evaluation of both the reasoning process and the proposed response.

EvidenceWhat it demonstrates
Evidence-level disciplineSeparated historical TDSB illustrations, Ontario-wide human-rights findings, local TVDSB strategy commitments, comparative public practice, and capstone synthesis.
Scope containmentNarrowed a broad anti-racism concern into a focused contribution on role clarity, institutional readiness, pathway protection, sustainability, and accountability.
Anti-deficit synthesisRefused problem definitions that positioned Black students as lacking resilience, motivation, aspiration, or readiness.
Prototype designTranslated findings into a role charter, feedback loop, pathway monitoring, readiness indicators, and evaluation questions while stating that the tools remain unpiloted.

Critical reflection

The most significant growth was learning that precision is not political retreat. There was an initial concern that narrowing the project would weaken its structural critique; instead, clear boundaries made the critique harder to dismiss. This meant learning to say what the evidence establishes, what it suggests, and what it cannot yet prove. Creative problem-solving emerged through translation: moving from broad principles to practical mechanisms without pretending that a prototype is a tested solution.

Continuing development

Next is deepening formal evaluation design, including baseline construction, indicator validation, mixed-method analysis, and participatory interpretation of findings.

9.4 · Interprofessional Collaboration

Collaboration as more than cordial participation

START 3 → FINAL 4

This competency requires coordinated work across roles and forms of expertise to reach sound decisions about complex health and social issues.

EvidenceWhat it demonstrates
Mentor transitionRestated the project, preserved continuity, and adapted communication as mentorship moved from Rheba Moore-Nash to Colette Greaves.
Mentor-guided refinementUsed contextual feedback to contain scope, clarify intended users, and strengthen the distinction between conceptual contribution and local evaluation.
Capstone ConferenceTranslated the project for an interdisciplinary academic audience and participated in shared knowledge exchange.
Collaborative implementation designBuilt student, family, community, coach, guidance, leadership, data, and accountability perspectives into the framework rather than treating one profession as the sole source of knowledge.

Critical reflection

The capstone was a reminder that collaboration requires visible purpose, shared language, role clarity, and an ability to preserve the core argument while allowing others to shape its form. The mentor transition tested this directly: the work had to be made legible, guidance invited, and progress kept moving without assigning one person responsibility for it. It also clarified that Black students, families, and communities must be treated as knowledge holders within future co-development, not as stakeholders invited only after the design is complete.

Continuing development

The next phase should establish a co-development table with explicit community authority, decision rules, compensation, confidentiality protections, and a documented response to contributions.

9.5 · Self-Awareness & Reflexivity

Holding conviction and evidence at once

START 3 → FINAL 4

This competency requires honest examination of strengths, limitations, values, emotions, and positionality, and the use of that insight to improve communication, judgment, and professional development.

EvidenceWhat it demonstrates
Positionality statementMade visible how personal identities and professional history shaped the questions, urgency, and interpretation.
D.E.A.L. reflectionUsed Description, Examination, and Articulation of Learning to analyze mentor transition, scope, evidence boundaries, and leadership choices.
Transparent limitationsNamed the absence of local outcome data, systematic review methods, human-subjects research, and pilot testing.
Future development planIdentified evaluation design, participatory co-development, local baseline work, and pilot testing as necessary next capabilities.

Critical reflection

The strongest asset was moral and conceptual clarity. The risk was urgency: the desire to address the full architecture of anti-Black racism could have produced a project too broad to defend or use. Reflexivity helped distinguish conviction from evidence and ambition from scope. It also required acknowledging that proximity does not remove the need for method — it increases the obligation to make interpretation transparent. The capstone ends with a stronger capacity to hold a firm justice position while inviting scrutiny of the reasoning behind it.

Continuing development

Reflective writing will continue, alongside seeking critical feedback from Black students and community scholars, and building more explicit checkpoints where assumptions, power, and unintended consequences are examined before implementation decisions are finalized.
