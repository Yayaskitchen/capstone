The inquiry brought together four evidence layers, plus mentor-guided application. Each layer served a different analytical purpose, and the project maintained clear boundaries between them.

Analytical process

  1. Identify recurring patterns across scholarship, rights-based analysis, local strategy, and comparative practice.
  2. Separate student-facing functions from system-facing responsibilities.
  3. Organize the patterns into five connected domains.
  4. Translate the domains into implementation mechanisms, tools, and evaluation questions.
  5. Test every claim against the evidence boundary: historical illustration, provincial finding, local commitment, or capstone synthesis.

Why this work matters

Carl James and Tana Turner's analysis of the schooling of Black students in the Greater Toronto Area demonstrates how inequity is produced along educational pathways, not only at the point of graduation. In historical TDSB data reported by James, 79 percent of Black students had graduated or accumulated sufficient credits after five years, compared with 87 percent of white students and 90 percent of other racialized students. Thirteen percent of Black students had left school, compared with 8 percent and 6 percent respectively (James, 2021, pp. 55–56). These figures are historical, single-board illustrations — not current Ontario or TVDSB estimates.

The pathway concern extends beyond graduation status. James also reports sharp inequities in gifted identification and postsecondary application patterns. The analytical lesson is not that Black students lack capacity or aspiration. It is that institutional expectations, course access, identification practices, discipline, and guidance decisions can narrow future choice long before a final graduation statistic is recorded.

The Ontario Human Rights Commission's 2025 action plan confirms that the issue is both longstanding and systemic. It calls for outcome-based accountability, standardized and disaggregated monitoring, and attention to belonging and well-being alongside achievement, discipline, and graduation. TVDSB's own strategy links anti-Black racism with mental, emotional, and physical health and recognizes that Black students require environments that affirm identity and support holistic thriving.

Belonging is not a soft outcome. It is part of the infrastructure that makes learning possible. Capstone proposition

Methodological limits

  • No local student-level administrative data were analyzed.
  • No formal interviews or human-subjects research are claimed as part of the evidence base.
  • The literature review was applied and purposive, not systematic.
  • The environmental scan was limited to public materials and cannot establish implementation quality or effectiveness.
  • The framework and proposed tools remain prototypes requiring co-development and piloting.
Claim discipline

The portfolio uses the term findings to mean findings from the synthesis. It does not use the term to imply empirical findings about TVDSB students, staff, or program outcomes.
