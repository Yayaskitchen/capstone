Section 10 · Final critical self-reflection

Joining moral clarity with disciplined form

The most significant learning

Justice-oriented work must be ambitious in purpose and disciplined in form.

The capstone began with a broad and urgent concern: Black students continue to encounter educational environments in which their belonging, capabilities, behaviour, and futures are interpreted through racialized institutional expectations. The early aim was to name that system without compromise. What became clear is that naming the system is only the beginning — a credible applied contribution must also specify the evidence, the mechanism, the actors, the boundaries, the implementation conditions, and the measures through which change can be examined.

The project's strongest conceptual shift was from asking only how Graduation Coaches support Black students to asking whether the institution is prepared for Black students. That distinction changed the work. It prevented the coach from being framed as a compensatory service around an unchanged system, and it prevented Black student flourishing from being reduced to individual resilience, attendance, credits, or graduation. Flourishing came to mean belonging, agency, dignity, identity affirmation, learning, well-being, achievement, leadership, joy, and future possibility. The coach remains essential, but the institutional obligation cannot be delegated to the coach.

Collaborative learning shaped that development. Rheba Moore-Nash supported the early direction of the capstone before going on leave. The transition to Colette Greaves required clarifying the purpose and making the project more coherent for a new mentoring relationship — a lesson that continuity does not happen automatically. The work must be legible enough that another person can enter it, challenge it, and help move it forward. Mentor engagement also helped distinguish what mattered from what was feasible. The project became stronger when it became more contained: scope was not a retreat from justice, but the condition that made the contribution usable and defensible.

Strengths, critique, and continuing responsibility

The strengths of this capstone were structural analysis, synthesis across bodies of knowledge, and the ability to translate philosophical commitments into practical institutional questions. Carl James revealed how racialization operates through expectations, streaming, discipline, identification, and pathway restriction. bell hooks grounded the work in voice, care, dialogue, and whole-person learning. Marimba Ani pushed the analysis beyond inclusion to institutional worldview — whose knowledge, behaviour, history, and aspirations are treated as legitimate? The Ontario Human Rights Commission then anchored the work in duty, accountability, monitoring, and remedy. These sources did not decorate the project; they changed what the project was able to see and what it refused to normalize.

The central critique is that the capstone does not establish local effectiveness. No TVDSB administrative data were analyzed, no systematic review or human-subjects research was undertaken, and the framework and tools have not been co-developed, piloted, or validated. Those limits matter: the portfolio presents findings from the synthesis, not findings about TVDSB outcomes. The absence of local data is not evidence that inequity is absent, but it does constrain the claims that can be made. The next stage requires a privacy-protected local baseline, participatory co-development, a pilot, mixed-method evaluation, disaggregated pathway measures, and a documented assessment of institutional response.

Across the five competencies, this capstone produced a more integrated form of practice: health-equity expertise deepened by treating schools as health-producing institutions; systems thinking moved from identifying actors to specifying feedback, authority, escalation, and accountability; critical reasoning became more disciplined through scope containment and clear evidence boundaries; interprofessional collaboration strengthened through mentor transition, institutional engagement, and knowledge translation; and reflexivity deepened through examining how identity, professional experience, urgency, and commitments shaped both the power and the risks of the work.

This learning carries forward into consulting, policy, education, and leadership — toward work that moves beyond recommendations toward implementation structures that make responsibility visible, continuing to link human rights, community knowledge, data, governance, and accountability. Most importantly: Black students should not have to prove that they belong or survive harmful systems through extraordinary resilience. The work is to build institutions prepared to recognize, affirm, protect, and advance them.

The institution must become prepared to learn from Black students, accountable for how it responds, and worthy of the futures it helps shape. Final reflection
